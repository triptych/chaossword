# Chapter 1: Village Tides

The fishing nets weighed Lyra's arms down as she hauled them across the weathered dock. Salt-crusted ropes bit into her palms, but she'd long stopped noticing such things. What she did notice was the horizon—that perfect line where sea met sky, promising something beyond Stillwater's predictable rhythms. *There must be more than this. More than mending nets and gutting fish until my hands are permanently stained with scales.*

Gulls circled overhead, their raucous cries as familiar as her own thoughts. The morning sun cast long shadows across Stillwater's jumbled collection of wooden buildings, their thatched roofs silver with dew. Lyra paused, adjusting her grip on the nets. From this vantage point, she could see the entire village—the communal ovens already sending plumes of smoke into the pale blue sky, the boats gently rocking against their moorings, and the villagers beginning their day with the same motions they'd made yesterday and would make tomorrow.

"Lyra! Stop daydreaming and bring those nets!" Her father's voice carried across the dock, weathered and rough like everything else in Stillwater.

She quickened her pace, the worn planks creaking beneath her bare feet. Her father, Thom Nightwind, stood knee-deep in water beside his boat, his face already reddened by the early morning sun and salt spray. He didn't look up as she approached, focused on securing a loose plank.

"Sorry," she murmured, though she knew he barely registered her presence anymore. As the middle of five children, Lyra had mastered the art of fading into the background. Her older brothers had already apprenticed to fishermen in neighboring villages, while her younger sisters still commanded attention with their childish needs and demands. Lyra existed in the space between—useful but unremarkable.

She handed over the nets and stepped back, waiting for further instructions that didn't come. Her father had already moved on to the next task, his mind on the day's catch rather than his middle daughter. Lyra lingered a moment longer before turning away.

"I'm going to help Merina this morning," she called over her shoulder. Her father grunted in acknowledgment, which was as close to permission as she was likely to get.

The path to the oracle's hut wound away from the docks, climbing slightly up the gentle slope that protected Stillwater from the worst of the sea storms. Wild herbs grew alongside it—rosemary and sage that her mother, Elinna, harvested for her healing tinctures. Lyra plucked a sprig of lavender as she walked, rolling it between her fingers and inhaling the sharp, clean scent.

Merina's hut stood apart from the others, its weathered walls hung with wind chimes fashioned from shells and driftwood. The delicate sounds created a boundary between the ordinary world and the oracle's domain. Lyra hesitated at the threshold, as she always did. No matter how many times she visited, a small flutter of anticipation stirred in her chest.

"Come in, child. The water is already boiling." Merina's voice drifted from within before Lyra could announce herself. This no longer surprised her—the village oracle always seemed to know who approached her door before they knocked.

Lyra ducked inside, blinking as her eyes adjusted to the dimmer light. The interior smelled of herbs, sea salt, and beeswax candles. Merina sat cross-legged on a woven mat, her silver hair loose around her shoulders. Though the exact number of her years remained a mystery, her face was a map of fine wrinkles, particularly around her eyes—evidence of a life spent squinting at both sun and stars.

"Good morning," Lyra said, settling onto the mat across from her. "Father's taken the boat out early today. They're hoping for a better catch than yesterday."

Merina nodded, her gaze appearing both focused on Lyra and somehow distant. "The tide is more favorable today. The moon pulled strongly last night." She poured steaming water into two clay cups, dropping pinches of dried leaves into each. "But you didn't come to discuss fishing prospects."

It wasn't a question. Lyra cupped her hands around the warm tea, inhaling the fragrant steam. "I had the dream again."

Merina's eyes sharpened, her attention fully present now. "The same one? Water and fire?"

Lyra nodded, a chill running through her despite the warm tea in her hands. "I was drowning, but my skin burned as if I'd been thrown into a forge. And there was... a voice." She hesitated, struggling to capture the fading fragments of the dream. "Not words exactly, but a presence. Something ancient and angry."

The oracle was silent for a long moment, studying Lyra's face as if reading a complicated text. Finally, she sighed. "Dreams of opposing elements—water and fire, together—they speak of transformation, child. Great change."

"But what does it mean?" Lyra asked, leaning forward. This conversation had repeated itself over the past several weeks, but Merina's interpretations remained frustratingly vague.

The old woman smiled, the expression creating new constellations of wrinkles around her eyes. "It means your life will not always be mending nets and sorting herbs for me, though you do both admirably."

Lyra tried to hide her disappointment. She'd hoped for something more concrete—a direction, a sign of what path to take. At nineteen, she should have already chosen between joining the fishing fleet or training more formally as a healer under her mother. Instead, she drifted between roles, assisting where needed but committing to nothing.

"Here." Merina reached for a small wooden chest beside her. From it, she withdrew a smooth stone pendant on a leather cord. "The dreams grow stronger. You should wear this."

Lyra accepted the pendant. It was cool to the touch, deep blue with veins of silver running through it. "What is it?"

"Lapis lazuli. A stone of truth and protection." Merina's fingers, gnarled with age but still nimble, closed Lyra's hand around it. "It will help you remain anchored when the currents try to sweep you away."

Lyra slipped the cord over her head, feeling the weight of the stone settle against her chest. "Thank you." Though she wasn't certain she believed in the stone's power, Merina's gifts were rare and always thoughtfully chosen.

The morning passed in comfortable routine. Lyra ground herbs for Merina's remedies, sorted through the oracle's collection of shells and stones, and transcribed the old woman's observations about tides and lunar phases into the village records. It was peaceful work, requiring just enough attention to quiet her restless thoughts without fully engaging her mind.

Shortly after midday, their routine was interrupted by a commotion from the village center. Voices raised in excitement carried to the oracle's hut, along with the unfamiliar clanking of metal and stamping of hooves.

"Ah," Merina said, looking up from the dried seaweed she was sorting. "Merrick's early this season."

"The merchant?" Lyra set aside her mortar and pestle. Merrick's visits were infrequent but always brought welcome variety to village life—new goods, news from distant ports, and occasionally travelers with strange accents and stranger stories.

Merina nodded. "Go. Your curiosity is making your fingers clumsy anyway." There was no reproach in her tone, only fond understanding.

Lyra didn't need to be told twice. She rinsed her herb-stained hands in a basin of water and hurried outside, following the sounds of excitement to the village square.

Merrick's wagon was already surrounded by villagers, children darting between adults for a better view of the merchant's wares. The man himself stood beside his wagon, gesturing expansively as he spoke with the village elder. Tall and broad-shouldered, with a beard that contained more gray than when Lyra had last seen him, Merrick cut an imposing figure in his traveled-stained cloak.

Lyra joined the crowd, standing on tiptoe to see over shoulders. The wagon was laden with fabrics in colors rarely seen in Stillwater, tools of finer make than their local blacksmith could produce, and small luxuries like sugar and spices. But it was the conversation, not the goods, that caught her attention.

"...third village razed in as many months," Merrick was saying, his voice grave. "Blackwood's fleet grows bolder with each success."

The elder, a stooped man named Gareth, shook his head. "Surely the coastal patrol vessels offer some protection?"

Merrick laughed without humor. "The kingdoms squabble among themselves while pirates rule the sea lanes. Blackwood outmaneuvers the patrol ships at every turn."

"Blackwood?" Lyra found herself speaking before she could think better of it. "Captain Raven Blackwood?"

Heads turned, and Lyra immediately regretted drawing attention to herself. But Merrick regarded her with interest.

"You know of the Pirate Queen, girl?" he asked.

Lyra felt heat rise to her cheeks. "Only stories. They say her ship is faster than any in the kingdoms, and that she never leaves survivors."

"Not stories enough, it seems." Merrick's expression darkened. "The rumors of her cruelty fall short of the truth. But there's something new driving her raids." He lowered his voice, though in the crowded square, many could still hear. "They say she seeks artifacts of power—ancient weapons and relics. She questions survivors before..." He glanced at the children in the crowd and left the sentence unfinished.

A strange chill washed over Lyra, reminiscent of her dreams—that sensation of burning while drowning. Something about Merrick's words resonated with a half-forgotten memory. Hadn't Merina once mentioned a legend about a powerful weapon hidden near Stillwater generations ago?

"What sort of artifacts?" she asked, ignoring the disapproving glance from Elder Gareth at her continued interruption.

Merrick shrugged. "Old magic. The kind wise folk leave buried." He turned back to his wagon, clearly considering the topic closed. "Now, who's interested in finest thread from the Eastern Kingdoms? Or perhaps some medicinal herbs impossible to grow in your salt-laden soil?"

The crowd's attention shifted back to commerce, but Lyra stood rooted in place, her hand unconsciously rising to touch the lapis lazuli pendant at her throat. The burning water of her dreams suddenly seemed less abstract, more ominous.

As if sensing her thoughts, a cool breeze swept in from the sea, carrying the tang of salt and the distant cry of gulls. Lyra turned to look back toward the horizon, that endless line between known and unknown. For the first time, the sight brought more anxiety than longing.

*There must be more than this*, she had thought that morning. Now, watching the bustling activity around Merrick's wagon, she wondered if "more" might come in forms she wasn't prepared to face.

Overhead, storm clouds gathered on the distant horizon, though the sky above Stillwater remained clear. Like everything else about this day, it felt like a warning—or perhaps a promise—of changes to come.
